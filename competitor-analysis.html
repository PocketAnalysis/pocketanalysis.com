What should you compare?

Start with one decision, one audience and a defined area. A local cleaning business, for example, may need to compare a recurring home-cleaning offer with other providers serving the same area. Comparing every cleaning service in the UK would answer a different question.

  • Customer and service: who the offer serves, where it is available and what work is included.
  • Price basis: hourly, per visit, per project or subscription; minimum bookings and additional charges.
  • Positioning: the benefits the business emphasises and the evidence supporting them.
  • Buying journey: how customers enquire, book and understand the offer.
  • Evidence date: when a page was checked and whether a price or claim is still available.

Turn a price into a fair comparison

A lower headline price does not necessarily mean a lower total cost. Compare the same duration, frequency, scope and conditions. Record missing information instead of guessing it.

For illustration, an offer of £20 per hour with a three-hour minimum starts at £60 per visit. An offer of £25 per hour with a two-hour minimum starts at £50. These are hypothetical examples, not current supplier prices. They show why the minimum visit matters as much as the hourly rate.

The next action might be to make your own inclusions and minimum booking clearer. The comparison alone cannot prove what customers will pay or which offer will win.

Competitor research and market research answer different questions

Competitor research examines available alternatives and their positioning. Market research adds wider context, such as dated official statistics, customer needs and local conditions. A large national market figure is not the same as demand for your specific offer in your area.

Use public information as a starting point. Customer conversations, enquiries and small measured tests help you check whether a proposed change actually solves a problem.

A simple research process you can use

  1. Write the decision: for example, “Should I change my minimum booking?”
  2. Choose comparable competitors: businesses with a similar customer, service and geography.
  3. Record evidence: links, dates, prices, inclusions and any missing details.
  4. Separate fact from interpretation: a published price is an observation; a reason customers prefer it needs further evidence.
  5. Choose a next step: ask customers, clarify a service page or test one change, then measure the result.

What a report can and cannot tell you

Sources and dates make the work easier to check. Public offers may not be directly comparable, search metrics can be unavailable and scenarios remain assumptions.
